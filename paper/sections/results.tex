\subsection{Analysing Features}\label{ss:sae_feat_analysis}

    \caption{

We visualise latent activations over all $100\times100$ ordered bigrams $(d_1, d_2)$ using heatmaps (Figure \ref{fig:sae-heatmap}). We exclude the 8\% of latents that activate on fewer than 0.1\% of inputs. The remaining latents fall into two categories: $k$-symbol detectors (91\% of all latents) and special latents (2 latents, or 1.6\%).
Most latents are $1$-symbol detectors (88\%). They activate only if one specific symbol is present at either $d_1$ or $d_2$, producing a `plus-sign' pattern in Figure \ref{fig:sae-heatmap}. They activate most strongly when $d_1=d_2$.
3\% of latents detect multiple symbols ($k>1$) in this way simultaneously, showing multiple `plus-signs'.
Only two latents (11 and 56) do not follow this pattern. Latent 11 activates on 64\% of inputs and latent 56 on 9.9\%, compared to fewer than 4.5\% for all other latents (Appendix \ref{app:further_analysis}). Because they activate across many different inputs, they cannot simply indicate that one specific symbol is present.

Moreover, latent 11 strongly correlates with $\Delta \alpha$ (Pearson $r=0.807$ over the whole dataset). Latent 56 has a weaker negative correlation ($r=-0.3213$). All other latents have $|r| < 0.004$. This gap distinguishes latents 11 and 56 from the symbol detectors and suggests that they may encode ordering information. We use $|r| = 0.3$ as a threshold for identifying special latents because of the significant gap between these latents and others, but other $|r| \in [0.1, 0.5]$ provide similar results.

Since $\Delta \alpha$ is positive when attention is weighted towards $d_1$, we refer to latent 11 as strongly $d_1$-favouring and latent 56 as weakly $d_2$-favouring (see correlation details in Appendix \ref{app:further_analysis}).

\subsection{Steering Experiments}\label{ss:steering_exp}

Steering latent 11 swaps for 50\% of model outputs (Figure \ref{fig:steering_feat_success}). There are several failure reasons (visualisations in Appendix \ref{app:further_steering}):
\textbf{No activation (36\%)}: Some inputs do not activate latent 11 ($m=0$).
\textbf{Negative scaling required (9.2\%)}: Swapping $o_1$ requires negative $p$, which we reject as BatchTopK activations are non-negative by construction.
\textbf{Mutually exclusive swap ranges (2.3\%)}: Each output position can be individually swapped but not simultaneously.
\textbf{No swap in observed range (1.8\%)}: One logit remains dominant across all tested $p$.
\textbf{Matching bigram elements (0.8\%)}: $d_1 = d_2$, making the swap degenerate.

It is sometimes possible to scale a co-activating non-special latent to change the logit distribution and successfully swap more outputs with latent 11. Therefore, 50\% is not the ceiling (see Appendix \ref{app:further_steering}).
Steering latent 56 swaps 2.0\% of outputs and never coactivates with latent 11, suggesting they may be complementary.
We additionally test steering non-special latents and do not find comparable behaviour (Appendix \ref{app:further_steering}). This suggests that the effect is specific to special latents.

Across inputs, logits at $o_1$ scale approximately linearly with $p$ (Figure \ref{fig:steering_feat_success}) and linear regression achieves mean $R^2>0.999$. We therefore estimate exact swap thresholds at $o_1$ analytically using lines fitted to the $d_1$ and $d_2$ logits.

\subsection{Generalisation to other SAEs}\label{ss:sae_generalisation}

Across 2,613 SAEs, high-performing ones frequently contain special latents, which we identify using $\Delta \alpha$ correlation $|r| > 0.3$ (visualised in Appendix \ref{app:further_generalise}, Figure \ref{fig:special_latents_vs_performance}).

We implement a pipeline to identify special latents and steer them to swap model outputs (Appendix \ref{app:further_generalise}). We run this pipeline on six selected high-performing checkpoints (Appendix \ref{app:exp_setup}, Table \ref{tab:selected-saes}).
All six checkpoints learned at least one special latent. Five learned a pair of opposing special latents, one $d_1$-favouring and one $d_2$-favouring. Steering at least one special latent swapped a substantial proportion of outputs, ranging from $26.6\%$ to $72.5\%$ of the dataset (Appendix \ref{app:further_generalise}, Table \ref{tab:steering_generalisation}). Conditional on latent activation, these rates were substantially higher. Swap failures primarily occur for the same reasons as in Section \ref{ss:steering_exp}.
This suggests that steerable graded latents are not unique to a single BatchTopK checkpoint, although the limited subset of evaluated SAEs limits statistical significance.
Further results are in Appendix \ref{app:further_generalise}.
