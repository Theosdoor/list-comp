\section{Introduction}

Transformers can learn mechanisms for relational composition \citep{wattenberg2024relational}, in which multiple feature vectors are composed into one fixed-length vector while preserving the relation between them. For example, \citet{farrell2025order} train a transformer on an ordered bigram copying task that learns a relative magnitude-based composition mechanism. The transformer does not allocate separate directions for $(x,y)$ and $(y,x)$. Instead, it uses shared embedding directions and changes their coefficients according to the attention pattern.

This poses an interesting challenge for sparse autoencoders (SAEs) \citep{bricken2023monosemanticity, huben2024sparse} and their variants \citep{gao2024scaling, bussmann2024batchtopk, rajamanoharan2024jumping, bussmann2025learning, karvonen2025saebench, leask2025inference}. 
If the transformer reuses shared embedding directions and varies their relative magnitudes, then an SAE trained on these activations may recover the shared directions and encode order in activation magnitude.
We call these \emph{graded latents}, for which different activation magnitudes carry different information and latent on/off activation alone is insufficient to fully understand it.
This matters for practical SAE workflows because many feature dashboards label a latent by inspecting top-activating examples or by checking whether it fires on a class of inputs.
A latent whose low, medium and high activations correspond to different relational states could look broadly active or noisy under that workflow, even when its activation value tracks a coherent internal variable.
This challenges interpretation methods that primarily treat SAE latents as on/off feature presence indicators \citep{paulo2025automatically, quirke2025binary}. The issue persists even if an SAE successfully recovers the underlying feature directions, separating it from previous work focused on dictionary correctness \citep{chanin2024absorption, leask2025sparse}.

For example, \citet{chanin2024absorption} find that using sparsity as a proxy can cause feature absorption and \citet{leask2025sparse} find that SAEs do not find a unique and complete dictionary of atomic features.

However, \citet{farrell2025order} did not study the impact on SAEs.
In this paper, we train a suite of BatchTopK \cite{bussmann2024batchtopk}, JumpReLU \cite{rajamanoharan2024jumping} and Matryoshka \cite{bussmann2025learning} SAEs on the toy transformer from \citet{farrell2025order}. In this toy setting, high-performing SAEs frequently learn latents whose activation values correspond to different bigram orderings.

In Section \ref{s:method} we describe the transformer task, SAE training setup, and graded latent identification and steering method.
In Section \ref{s:results}, we identify two graded SAE latents using correlations with $\Delta \alpha$, the difference between the separator token's attention to the first and second input symbols. We then show through steering interventions that scaling these latents can swap the model output from $(x,y)$ to $(y,x)$.
Across a broad SAE sweep, high-performing SAEs tend to contain latents whose activations correlate with $\Delta \alpha$. For a selected set of six high-performing BatchTopK, JumpReLU, and Matryoshka SAEs, steering these latents swaps between 25--73\% of the transformer's original outputs. We treat this as a controlled case study rather than evidence that the same behaviour occurs in large pretrained models, and use it to motivate further investigation into graded latents in settings such as Gemma Scope \citep{lieberum2024gemma, mcdougall2025gemmascope2}.
